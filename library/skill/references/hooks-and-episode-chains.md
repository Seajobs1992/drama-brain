# Hooks and episode chains

## Choose the audience's question

A useful opening combines a legible situation with an unresolved consequence. Pick the mechanism that suits the genre rather than forcing every lead to hide wealth or power.

| Mechanism | Opening situation | What must follow |
|---|---|---|
| Audience knows more | A feared commander is treated as a servant | Increasing consequences of the mistaken judgement, then a meaningful recognition |
| Audience knows less | A nurse destroys the only apparent antidote | Evidence that makes the act intriguing rather than arbitrary confusion |
| Immediate danger | A lift stops while water rises | Clear geography, an achievable goal and worsening constraints |
| Contradiction | A bride recognises the officiant as the man she buried | A concrete clue and a consequential response |
| Moral pressure | A witness must accuse her brother to save someone | A real cost on both sides of the decision |
| Intimacy at risk | A lover receives a message intended for someone else | A character-specific interpretation and a choice |

Open near the consequential action. Establish enough identity, desire and stakes to understand it. Do not impose a universal three-second formula or identical beat percentages. Allocate time according to the brief and test the actual sequence.

## Escalation must change something

Increase pressure through a new constraint, public exposure, loss of an ally, compromised evidence, a moral cost, or a narrowing escape. Repeating the same insult more loudly is not a new beat.

A useful causal chain is: because the character does X, another person does Y, which forces a more expensive choice. If the sequence only connects with “and then”, identify what actually drives it.

In a humiliation story, make disrespect specific and let the protagonist retain motives and agency. Explain why they tolerate it, what would make them act, and what restraint costs them. An invincible lead still needs something power cannot secure: trust, belonging, forgiveness or freely chosen love.

## Reveal, proof and reaction

Separate the claim from the evidence that makes it credible. A declaration of identity may be challenged; a distinctive object, corroborated history or consequential action can support it. Plant the relevant clue before its payoff when the reveal depends on recognition.

Plan three things:
1. What new fact is exposed?
2. Who recognises it first, and what visible behaviour changes?
3. What action becomes possible or unavoidable afterward?

The reaction is part of the payoff. Do not cut to every character automatically. Select reactions that carry different information: disbelief, recognition, calculation or shame. A facial close-up with no changed understanding is decorative coverage.

## Cliffhanger choices

| Ending type | Cut point | Next opening obligation |
|---|---|---|
| Imminent consequence | A decisive action is committed, outcome unseen | Show the outcome and its cost |
| Recognition | Someone identifies a person or object | Establish why that recognition matters |
| Reversal | Apparent victory exposes a new disadvantage | Demonstrate the altered situation |
| Forced choice | The character must choose between meaningful losses | Let the decision or its obstruction occur |
| Emotional rupture | A confession or rejection changes the relationship | Show the other person's consequential response |
| Discovery | A specific clue contradicts the current explanation | Investigate or act on it |

Do not end on a random entrance, ringtone or facial reaction unless its meaning is already clear. Ask: “What exact result is the viewer waiting to see?” If the answer is merely “what happens next”, strengthen the cause and stakes.

Pay off some promises before opening others. Excessive postponement teaches the audience that endings will not deliver. Do not undo a clear event through an unseeded dream, fake death or missing context solely to extend the story.

## Episode handoffs

Record: previous final action, unresolved consequence, next first action, new information and continuity state. Repeat only enough footage or dialogue to orient the viewer. Avoid restarting the same confrontation without progress.

A finale can close the central promise. It need not manufacture another cliffhanger when the requested series is finished.

## Original worked example

Brief: a 60-second medical mystery episode, no violence. A junior nurse is blamed for stealing a sealed medicine vial.

- 0–8 seconds: The senior doctor locks the medicine cabinet. Through its glass, the nurse sees the supposedly stolen vial. Hook: why is he hiding what he says is missing?
- 8–22: She asks to check the batch label. He orders her to leave; a colleague starts recording. Private suspicion becomes public risk.
- 22–38: She produces the delivery slip. Its number differs from the cabinet label. This episode delivers concrete evidence, not merely an accusation.
- 38–52: The doctor reaches for the vial. The nurse blocks the cabinet door and says, “That batch was recalled.” Keep the delivery playable within the action window.
- 52–60: A patient monitor sounds offscreen. The colleague looks toward the ward: “He already gave her a dose.” End on the nurse deciding to run, with the cabinet and proof left behind.
- Next opening: Stay with her arrival at the patient's bedside. Show a specific condition and a response. Do not jump to unrelated backstory or claim the alarm was meaningless.

These timings are an illustrative editorial budget, not supported generation durations or a clinical treatment guide. The mystery concerns concealment and accountability. Any detailed medical procedure needs appropriate research.

## Measured patterns from 14 films

Source: every episode of The Untouchable Mechanic and the 13 Multi-Genre Study films (about 630 episodes), read on 2026-11-20 (`research/CROSS-FILM-PLAYBOOK.md`). These are tendencies with ranges, not formulas. They show how the stories are built, not why they were watched.

### Handoffs and timing
- **The next episode picks up the cliffhanger within about 4 s in 59% of handoffs**, ranging from 33% to 72% by film. Fight and danger endings are answered at once. Delays are deliberate: at arc boundaries, on romance threads, and after "plan" episodes. Replaying the last line before answering is rare (0 to 6 times per film).
- **The key turn lands just past the middle of an episode,** at a median of about 53% (45% to 61% by film), leaving room to build the ending.
- **Typical episodes run about 70 s.** Opening episodes run much longer (often 120 to 180 s) before lengths settle.
- **The weakest openings are ordinary:** scenery, an errand, polite excuses. The strongest open in danger, in dramatic irony, or on the answer to the last cliffhanger.

### Cliffhanger mix
Across about 580 endings:

| Type | Share |
|---|---|
| Danger | 24% |
| Reveal pending | 21% |
| Decision | 17% |
| Arrival | 10% |
| Closure | 9% |
| Humiliation pending | 7% |
| Identity claim | 4% |
| Romance withheld | 4% |
| False resolution | 3% |

No film relies on one type, and the mix follows the phase of the story:
- **Early:** humiliation and arrival.
- **Middle and climax:** danger.
- **After big wins:** decision and closure.
- **Before a twist:** a false resolution.

Action and fantasy lean on danger. Romance and family lean on decisions and withheld reveals.

### Mechanisms most hits share
1. **The viewer knows first** (13 of the 14 films). The pleasure is watching characters stay wrong. When the narrator learns things with the viewer, the title or premise has to supply the certainty instead ("it was always me").
2. **Proof before the insult.** Show the lead's worth before anyone looks down on them, so mistreatment reads as anticipation.
3. **One object carries the mystery and changes meaning** at least twice (a pendant, a cufflink, a memory card, a toy recording).
4. **Pressure climbs by changing kind, not volume:** higher authorities, a new exclusion after each win, a different kind of threat every few episodes.
5. **Payback is public and exact:** witnesses who change sides, and the same words or gesture returned.
6. **Each insult chooses the arena where the lead later wins.**
7. **A visible scale or count:** a step counter, children counted one by one, a countdown.
8. **Care before reward;** love proven by a cost.
9. **A child or an animal says what the adults cannot.**
10. **Comfort is built, then broken,** so the next threat has something to take.
11. **Apologies are tested on screen;** saving someone stays separate from forgiving them.
12. **Absence proves worth:** when the lead leaves, the others fail at what she did.

### Narration and inner voice
- **A narrated line in the first seconds can state the wound or the impossible choice** before any scene plays (the push off the cliff; "my father could only take one of us").
- **Inner thoughts that contradict actions keep the viewer ahead** (a tormentor who privately cares; a villain thinking his plan).
- **A film narrated throughout loses dramatic irony.** Never use narration to report big events that should be seen.

### What the films get wrong
Avoid these:
- the same beat for 3 to 6 episodes without a new cost
- stakes and weapons dropped
- proofs and powers that were never set up
- big turns told, not shown
- names, ranks and clocks that drift
- coercion presented as romance
